# V2X, Inc. (NYSE: VVX) — Sponsor LBO Model

V2X is a scaled, diversified government-services prime with multi-year, largely cost-reimbursable and T&M contract revenue, negative net working capital, and capex under 0.5% of revenue — the kind of low-capital-intensity, high-cash-conversion profile a sponsor can lever without betting on a turnaround. The post-2022 Vectrus/Vertex combination is still working through integration, and FY2023-FY2025 results show EBITDA margin climbing from roughly 6.0% to 6.9% as duplicative SG&A comes out and the combined backlog is cross-sold across both legacy customer bases — a trend a sponsor can underwrite further without needing new business wins. Taken private, the company avoids the quarterly scrutiny that a still-recovering, thin-margin GAAP earnings profile invites from public holders, while a disciplined owner keeps pushing the same margin and working-capital levers management has already demonstrated.

The model underwrites entry at 11.0x TEV/FY2025A EBITDA of $306.8mm ($3,374.7mm TEV), a modest premium to V2X's current undisturbed trading multiple of roughly 10.5x and in line with the KBR/LinQuest benchmark of 11.0x, sitting toward the lower half of the 10.5x-13.3x range disclosed in Perspecta's 2021 fairness opinion for comparable government-services take-privates. Exit in FY2030 is underwritten at the same 11.0x — the base case assumes no multiple re-rating, so the return is earned, not bought. New debt at close totals 6.0x EBITDA ($1,840.8mm: a 4.5x Term Loan B at 7.00% and a 1.5x senior notes tranche at 8.50%), funded alongside a $1,680.6mm sponsor equity check that is roughly half of entry TEV.

Base case returns: 2.32x MOIC and an 18.3% IRR over the five-year hold, with total leverage delevering from 5.3x to 2.0x Total Debt/EBITDA and interest coverage improving from 2.4x to 4.9x as the model's negative working capital and 75% cash sweep pay down the Term Loan B. The single assumption the return is most sensitive to is the entry multiple itself — the two-way sensitivity table shows IRR swinging from roughly 30% at 9.0x entry down to 11% at 13.0x entry (holding exit flat at 11.0x), a materially wider range than either the exit-multiple or revenue-CAGR axes, because leverage is sized off EBITDA turns rather than off the price paid, so every incremental turn of purchase price comes straight out of the sponsor's equity check.

The strongest argument against the deal is customer and contract concentration colliding with a thin margin structure: V2X's revenue sits almost entirely with the U.S. government, is billed through a relatively small number of large, periodically recompeted contracts, and carries GAAP EBITDA margins near 7% that leave little room to absorb a lost recompete, a novation dispute, or a continuing-resolution-driven funding gap. Layer 6.0x of new leverage on top of that, and Year-1 EBITDA/interest coverage of 2.4x is tight enough that a single material contract loss — not a broad market downturn — could pressure the covenant package before the deleveraging story has had time to play out. The return also assumes the post-merger margin expansion trend continues for five more years without a fresh catalyst; if it stalls at the FY2025 level instead, both the EBITDA-growth and deleveraging legs of the returns bridge weaken together.
